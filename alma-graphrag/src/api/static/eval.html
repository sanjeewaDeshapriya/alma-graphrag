Step 1 — Two evaluation routes

The system is tested in two independent ways. Route 1 checks rankings against an answer key built from rules. Route 2 checks rankings against what real people chose. The two routes answer different questions, use different data and are never averaged together; they share only the statistical tests.

Principal research question How can a live, weighted graph retrieval system model the linked and changing facts behind hotel choice, so that it produces explainable rankings of usable hotels that are better than filter-based and text-based search, and can its criterion weights be learned from human choices?

Route 1 query set & answer key

Every method receives the same traveller questions and the same saved reading of each question, so a difference in score comes from the ranking method only. The answer key is rule-based and graded: a hotel is fully relevant (2) when it meets every limit, partly relevant (1) when it misses a limit by no more than a tolerance band (price +15%, rating −0.2, star −1, travel time +2 min, disruption +3 min), and not relevant (0) otherwise. The bands stop any method's hard cut-off from acting as a perfect copy of the key.

Step 2 — The eight methods being compared

All methods rank the identical pool of 77 Colombo hotels and return a top-10 list. They differ only in what evidence they can see. Two floors (random, popularity) show whether the query matters at all; filter-and-sort stands for current practice on booking sites; four text methods stand for modern retrieval.

Random floor

Shuffles the pool. Any method that does not beat it is broken.

Popularity floor

Ranks by guest rating and ignores the question. Exposes benchmarks where the query does not matter.

Step 3 — How the score and the measures are worked out

The proposed method gives every usable hotel one score; the measures then check the top ten of each method's list against the answer key (Route 1) or against real choices (Route 2). All measures use k = 10 and are averaged over queries.

The hotel score

1 · Usable set F(q)

F(q) = { h ∈ H : c(h) = true for every hard limit c of q }, c(h) = false when the value is unknown

Remove before scoring: a hotel that breaks a hard limit cannot win its place back by scoring well on something else. If no hotel passes, the limits are relaxed and the answer says so.

2 · Combined score S(h | q)

S(h | q) = Σk wk(q) · xk(h), k ∈ {spatial, accessibility, facility, economic, disruption}, Σk wk = 1

xk(h) is the hotel's percentile position within F(q) on criterion k, from 0 to 1. Hand-set starting weights: spatial 0.25 · accessibility 0.20 · facility 0.25 · economic 0.15 · disruption 0.15.

Route 1 measures

Precision@10 (P@10)

P@10 = |R10 ∩ G| / 10

Of the ten hotels shown, the share that are relevant (grade 1 or 2).

Recall@10 (R@10)

R@10 = |R10 ∩ G| / |G|

Of all relevant hotels, the share in the top ten. With a typical 18 relevant hotels, the highest possible average is 0.632.

nDCG@10 headline

DCG@10 = Σi=1..10 gi / log2(i + 1) · nDCG@10 = DCG@10 / IDCG@10 · g ∈ {0, 1, 2}

Rewards putting fully relevant hotels near the top. IDCG@10 is the score of the best possible order.

MRR

MRR = 1 / (rank of the first relevant hotel)

How quickly the first good answer appears. 1.0 means it was ranked first.

Route 2 measures and comparisons

Hit@10

Hit@10 = share of choices whose chosen hotel is in the top 10

With one chosen hotel per task, R@10 equals Hit@10 and P@10 is capped at 1/10. A random order of 32 hotels gives 0.3125.

Choice nDCG@10 · average rank

choice nDCG@10 = 1 / log2(rank + 1) if rank ≤ 10, else 0

Rewards placing the chosen hotel high. Average rank is out of 32 (random = 16.5); lower is better.

Wins / ties / losses · effect size dz

dz = mean(Δ) / sd(Δ), Δ = nDCG@10proposed − nDCG@10other per query

Counts the queries each side wins, and how large the paired difference is.

Significance

paired bootstrap 95% range · Wilcoxon signed-rank · Holm correction, α = 0.05

Route 2 resamples whole participants (5,000 resamples), so choices by one person stay together.

Step 6 — Route 2: held-out human choices

Route 1 grades against rules we wrote. Route 2 grades against the hotel each participant chose in the discrete-choice study. It asks a different question: not “does the ranking meet the stated limits?” but “does it predict what people pick?”. Only the 28 test participants are scored; the 67 training participants are used only to fit weights.

Why both routes are reported. The rule-based key is strict about limits but cannot say which acceptable hotel a person prefers. The choice study can, but participants spent no real money, the study covers ten scenarios in Colombo, and only 95 of 247 people passed the attention and speed checks. Neither route replaces the other.
